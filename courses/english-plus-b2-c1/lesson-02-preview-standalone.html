Used To vs. Would for Past Habits

Two ways to talk about repeated past routines — and why one of them refuses to describe states.

1 Warm-up

Both "used to" and "would" describe repeated past habits or routines that no longer happen. They overlap a lot — but not completely.

1. "I used to live in Kraków" and "I would live in Kraków" — which is correct for describing a past state?

2. "Every summer, we would visit my grandmother" describes…

3. "Used to" can describe both repeated actions AND past states; "would" can only describe…

💡 If you can replace it with "was/were" (a state), only "used to" works. If it's a repeated action, either usually works.

3 The concept

Repeated actions vs. states

For repeated past actions, "used to" and "would" are interchangeable — either one works, and the choice is mostly stylistic ("would" often adds a storytelling feel). The real dividing line is states: "would" simply can't describe a past state (a feeling, belief, possession or ongoing condition) — only "used to" can.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Used to: used to + base verb — repeated actions AND states. "I used to smoke." / "I used to hate coffee."
✅ Would: would + base verb — repeated actions only, never states. "I would smoke a pack a day." ❌ "I would hate coffee."
🎯 The tricky part: stative verbs (believe, hate, own, know, be, live as a state, want) simply block "would" — no exceptions.

✅ "I used to own a motorbike." — a past state (possession). Only "used to" works.
❌ "I would own a motorbike." — ungrammatical; ownership isn't a repeated event, it's a continuous state.

When in doubt, ask: is this something that happened repeatedly and separately (would OK), or a continuous condition (would blocked, used to only)?

For a plain repeated action like "I used to cycle to work every day," either form works — "I would cycle to work every day" is just as correct. "Would" is simply the more common choice when you're narrating a story about the past, rather than just stating the fact.

4 Reading — Parkinson's Law and the Expansion of Work

Before the finance team tightened its deadlines, a single quarterly report used to take nearly two weeks to finish, even though the actual writing consumed barely two working days. According to Cyril Northcote Parkinson, the economist who first articulated this idea, work simply would expand to swallow whatever time was available for it — and nowhere was that clearer than in this one office.

Old-timers would recall how staff meetings would stretch on for two or three hours during quiet months, filling every free slot on the calendar. The office used to feel perpetually busy, even when there was demonstrably little to do, and deadlines that used to be vague and generous only encouraged the drift.

Everything changed when a new director imposed strict weekly deadlines and insisted that meetings run no longer than thirty minutes. Productivity, remarkably, barely suffered; the same reports that once occupied two weeks now got finished within days, seemingly confirming Parkinson's original observation with startling precision. The lesson, old staff admit, is uncomfortable: most of that old slack stemmed not from the work itself, but from how much room it was given to expand into.
