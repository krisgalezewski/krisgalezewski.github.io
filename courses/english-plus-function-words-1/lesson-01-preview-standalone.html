3 The concept

Four jobs, four forms

English pronouns change form depending on the job they do in the sentence. Is the person doing the action? Receiving it? Owning something? Or doing the action to themselves? Browse each type, then try Quick check — you'll see a sentence with a gap and decide which type of pronoun it needs.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Subject: I, you, he, she, it, we, they — before the verb, doing the action.
✅ Object: me, you, him, her, it, us, them — after a verb or a preposition (to, for, with, between…).
✅ Possessive: my / mine, your / yours, his, her / hers, its, our / ours, their / theirs — “my” + noun; “mine” alone.
✅ Reflexive: myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves — when the subject and object are the same person.
🎯 The tricky part: “myself” is not a polite version of “me”.

❌ “Please send any questions to myself.” → ✅ “Please send any questions to me.”
❌ “Myself and Tomasz will join the call.” → ✅ “Tomasz and I will join the call.”

Use a -self word only when it points back to the subject (“I blamed myself”), to add emphasis (“I fixed it myself”), or in “by myself” (= alone). And some verbs that need a reflexive in other languages don't in English: relax, concentrate, feel, meet, wonder — “I can't concentrate”, not “concentrate myself”.

4 Reading — How I Taught Myself Data Analysis

Two years ago, Maja was working in customer service and feeling stuck. Her manager kept telling her that the company needed people who could read data, but there was no budget for training. So she decided to teach herself. Every evening after work, she devoted an hour to free online courses, and at weekends she practised on real spreadsheets from her own team.

The first months were frustrating. “I kept comparing myself to colleagues who had studied statistics,” she says. “Their reports looked so professional, and mine looked like a school project.” What helped was a small study group she found online: four strangers who met on video calls, shared their mistakes and encouraged each other. Nobody was an expert, so nobody felt embarrassed asking basic questions.

The turning point came when Maja built a simple dashboard showing why customers were calling. She presented it to her manager, who immediately forwarded it to the director. Within six months, she and a colleague had moved into a new analytics role. Her advice for anyone in a similar position is straightforward: “Don't wait for someone to give you permission. Set yourself a small goal, stay consistent, and don't be afraid to share unfinished work — that's how people notice your potential.”
