Inversion with negative adverbials
When a sentence opens with a negative or restrictive adverbial, the auxiliary inverts — and the effect is dramatic
What is inversion?

Inversion means placing the auxiliary verb before the subject — the same word order as a question. When a sentence opens with a negative or restrictive adverbial, this inversion is obligatory in formal written English. It creates emphasis, drama, and a literary register.

Which auxiliaries invert?

The same auxiliaries used in question formation: be, have, do/does/did, will, would, can, could, should, must, might, may. If there is no auxiliary in the original sentence, add do / does / did.

Think of it like forming a question: "She will come." → "Will she come?" Inversion works the same way — the adverbial simply replaces the question word at the front.
Normal order vs inverted — with and without an existing auxiliary
Normal — has auxiliaryI had never seen such a sight before.
Inverted ✓Never before had I seen such a sight.
Normal — no auxiliaryShe rarely speaks in public.
Inverted — do added ✓Rarely does she speak in public.
Common mistakes ✗
Missing inversion ✗Never before I had seen such a sight. ✗ — the auxiliary must come before the subject, not after it
No auxiliary added ✗Rarely she speaks in public. ✗ — no auxiliary present, so "does" must be added: Rarely does she speak…
"No sooner…when" ✗No sooner had she arrived when it happened. ✗ — "no sooner" always pairs with than, never "when"
"Hardly…than" ✗Hardly had we sat down than the alarm rang. ✗ — "hardly / scarcely" always pairs with when or before, never "than"
"No sooner…than" and "Hardly/Scarcely…when/before" are never interchangeable. The paired conjunctions are fixed. Using the wrong one makes the sentence incorrect even if the inversion itself is perfect.
Not only…but also — inversion in the first clause only

With "not only…but also", inversion applies only to the first clause. The "but also" clause keeps normal subject-verb order.

Correct ✓Not only did she win the race, but she also broke the world record.
Wrong ✗Not only did she win the race, but also did she break the world record. ✗ — the second clause must NOT invert
The "but also" clause in "not only…but also" is a coordinated main clause and keeps normal order. Only the first, fronted clause inverts.
